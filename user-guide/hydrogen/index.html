    Hydrogen: Fuel Cell & Tank¶

    PhlyGreen models hydrogen-electric aircraft in two configurations: a pure fuel-cell ('Hydrogen') and a fuel-cell + battery hybrid ('FuelCellBattery'). The model is a physics-based PEM fuel-cell system (Systems/FuelCell/FuelCell.py) plus an optional cryogenic liquid-hydrogen tank (Systems/Tank/).

    1. The polarization curve¶

    The heart of the model is the cell voltage as a function of current density, computed with the analytical Kulikovsky / Massaro model (FuelCell.PolarizationCurve(i, P, T)). Starting from the open-circuit voltage \(V_{oc}\), three loss mechanisms are subtracted:

    \[ V_{\text{cell}}(j) = V_{oc} \;-\; \underbrace{R_{\Omega}\,j}_{\text{ohmic}} \;-\; \underbrace{\eta_{0}(j)}_{\text{activation + O$_2$ transport}} \;-\; \underbrace{V_{\text{conc}}(j)}_{\text{concentration}} \]
    • Ohmic loss \(R_\Omega j\) — membrane/contact resistance, linear in current density.
    • Activation + oxygen-transport loss \(\eta_0(j)\) — the Kulikovsky closed-form for the catalyst layer, built from the Tafel slope \(b\), the proton conductivity \(\sigma_t\), the catalyst-layer thickness \(l_t\), the reference O₂ concentration and the local oxygen diffusivity. The cathode oxygen concentration scales with the cathode pressure (\(c_h = c_{h,\text{ref}}\,p/p_{\text{ref}}\)), so running the cathode pressurized improves performance.
    • Concentration loss \(V_{\text{conc}}(j) = -B\ln(1 - j/j_{\text{lim}})\) — the steep drop as the limiting current density \(j_{\text{lim}}\) is approached.

    The cell and chemistry constants come from FC_Database (Systems/FuelCell/FC_Models.py, selected via fc_model). A database model holds only these electrochemical and operating parameters. The design choices of a stack (design cell voltage, stack power density, BoP mass ratio) are inputs of the design: v_cell_design, stack_power_density, bop_mass_ratio. Plot the curve as in example 20_hydrogen_fuel_cell.py:

    for j in (0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0):
    print(j, fc.PolarizationCurve(j, fc.Target_Press))

    2. Designing the stack — the role of the design voltage¶

    The fuel-cell stack is sized in ComputeAndStoreWeights(WTO). The key design knob is the design cell voltage \(V_{\text{cell,design}}\) (v_cell_design):

    1. the rated net power \(P_{\text{fc,rated}}\) follows from the design power-to-weight and WTO;
    2. the design current density is the point on the polarization curve at \(V_{\text{cell,design}}\) (solved with Brent), bounded by the rated current density i_rated;
    3. the surface power density is \(p_s = V_{\text{cell,design}}\cdot j_{\text{design}}\) [W/cm²], from which the total active area, the number of cells \(N_{\text{cells}}\) and the per-cell area \(A_{\text{cell}}\) are obtained;
    4. the stack mass follows from the area-specific power density (stack_power_density), and the balance-of-plant mass is bop_mass_ratio × stack, plus motor + PMAD.

    There is a genuine design trade: a higher design voltage runs each cell at a lower current density (locally more efficient), but needs more active area / a heavier stack to make the rated power — and a heavier powertrain needs more hydrogen, which needs a bigger stack, so the design can snowball. The result is a take-off-weight optimum at a moderate design voltage (very high voltages fail to close). Example 21_hydrogen_design_voltage_sweep.py sweeps it.

    energy = EnergyConfig(
    Ef=141.87e6,                    # hydrogen higher heating value [J/kg] (HHV basis, see below)
    eta_gearbox=0.96, eta_pmad=0.99, eta_electric_motor=0.96,
    fc_model='PEMFC_GoodPerformance',
    i_rated=2.5,                    # rated current density [A/cm^2]
    v_cell_design=0.5,              # design cell voltage [V]  <-- the key sizing knob
    stack_power_density=3000,       # area-specific stack power [W/kg]
    bop_mass_ratio=0.40,            # balance-of-plant / stack mass
)

    3. The air system (compressor) and net system efficiency¶

    PEM fuel cells run the cathode pressurized, which costs parasitic power. The air system (_compute_air_system_power) sizes a compressor to raise the ambient air to the cathode pressure and an expander that recovers part of the energy from the pressurized cathode exhaust:

    \[ \dot m_{\text{air}} \propto N_{\text{cells}}\,I\,\lambda, \qquad P_{\text{comp}} = \frac{\dot m_{\text{air}}\,c_{p}\,T_{\text{amb}}\,(\Pi^{0.286}-1)}{\eta_{\text{comp}}}, \]
    \[ P_{\text{turb}} = \eta_{\text{turb}}\,\dot m_{\text{exh}}\,c_{p,\text{exh}}\,T_{op}\, \Big(1 - \Pi_t^{-R/\bar c_{p,\text{exh}}}\Big), \qquad P_{\text{comp,net}} = P_{\text{comp}} - P_{\text{turb}} \]

    with stoichiometry \(\lambda\), pressure ratio \(\Pi = p_{\text{cathode}}/p_{\text{amb}}\) and expander ratio \(\Pi_t = 0.95\,p_{\text{cathode}}/p_{\text{amb}}\). The expander takes the cathode exhaust, not air (FuelCell.cathode_exhaust). Per mole of H₂ reacted it carries the nitrogen of the inlet air (\(0.5\lambda\cdot0.79/0.21\) mol), the oxygen left over (\(0.5(\lambda-1)\) mol), the water brought by the inlet humidity, and the product water that leaves as vapour (\(x_v\) mol; the liquid is separated before the expander). Its mass flow, heat capacity \(c_{p,\text{exh}}\) and isentropic exponent \(R/\bar c_{p,\text{exh}}\) (\(\bar c_p\) molar) are those of that mixture. At \(\lambda = 2\) and \(x_v = 1\) the exhaust carries slightly more mass than the inlet air and has a 13 % higher \(c_p\), so the expander recovers more power than an air expander would. The compressor takes the ambient air with its humidity. The compression demand grows with altitude (lower \(p_{\text{amb}}\)), so it is a real part of the operating-point dependence.

    The net system efficiency is the gross stack power minus the air-system (and PMAD) parasitics over the hydrogen chemical power:

    \[ \eta_{\text{sys}} = \frac{P_{\text{gross}} - P_{\text{comp,net}} - P_{\text{fixed}}}{\dot m_{\text{H}_2}\,\text{HHV}}, \qquad P_{\text{gross}} = N_{\text{cells}}\,V_{\text{cell}}\,I . \]

    Heating value. The low-temperature PEM stack is referenced to the higher heating value (HHV = 141.87 MJ/kg): the hydrogen chemical power is \(\dot m_{\text{H}_2}\,\text{HHV}\), with \(\dot m_{\text{H}_2}\) fixed by the stack current. The mission integrates this chemical power and the weight loop converts it to a mass as \(E / E_f\). Therefore Ef must be the HHV, 141.87e6 J/kg, as in examples/common.py. A lower-heating-value Ef (≈120 MJ/kg) would overstate the hydrogen mass by 18 %. The phase of the product water (vapour fraction \(x_v\), section 4) changes only the heat rejected to the coolant, not the hydrogen consumption.

    For the mission, ComputePRatio(alt, vel, P_net) solves for the operating current density that delivers the required net shaft power at the current flight condition and stores the stack hydrogen flow mdot_h2. The mission integrates the hydrogen chemical power \(\dot m_{H_2}\,\mathrm{HHV}\) of that flow, which also feeds the air system and the fixed auxiliaries. The take-off hydrogen power mission.TO_P_H2_Thermal is the same quantity at the take-off/OEI sizing point. The method also returns the ratio \(1/\eta_{\text{sys}}\), clipped to \(\eta_{\text{sys}} \in [0.01, 0.85]\); no result of the package is computed from it.

    4. Heat management¶

    A fuel cell is roughly half-efficient, so it rejects a large heat load. The heat the coolant must reject is the difference between the thermo-neutral voltage and the operating cell voltage. The thermo-neutral voltage depends on the phase in which the product water leaves the stack: 1.48 V if it leaves as liquid (HHV), 1.25 V if it leaves as vapour (LHV). Vapour carries its latent heat away with the cathode exhaust, so that heat never reaches the coolant. With \(x_v\) the fraction of the product water that leaves as vapour:

    \[ Q_{\text{thermal}} = N_{\text{cells}}\,I\,\Big(\frac{\Delta H_{HHV} - x_v\,(\Delta H_{HHV} - \Delta H_{LHV})}{2F} - V_{\text{cell}}\Big) \]

    Why product water at 80 °C can leave as vapour¶

    The 100 °C "boiling point" is the temperature at which the saturation pressure of water equals 1 atm, so that pure water boils. Water evaporates at any temperature into a gas mixture, provided the partial pressure of the vapour stays below the saturation pressure at that temperature (Dalton's law). This is why laundry dries at room temperature. The cathode channel of a fuel cell is exactly such a mixture: air flowing past the electrodes, into which the product water can evaporate.

    At the stack temperature \(T_{op}\) the gas can hold vapour up to the saturation partial pressure \(p_{sat}(T_{op})\). At a total cathode pressure \(P\), the largest vapour mole fraction is therefore \(y_{sat} = p_{sat}(T_{op})/P\). For the database stacks (\(T_{op}\) = 80 °C, \(P\) = 1.5 bar): \(p_{sat}\) = 47.3 kPa and \(y_{sat}\) = 0.315. That is, up to 31.5 % of the exhaust molecules can be water vapour.

    Whether that is enough depends on how much air flows through the cathode. This is set by the air stoichiometric ratio \(\lambda\) (FuelCell.stoich, default 2): the oxygen supplied divided by the oxygen the reaction consumes. \(\lambda\) = 1 would supply exactly the oxygen of the reaction; \(\lambda\) = 2 supplies twice that, and the excess leaves with the exhaust.

    Per mole of hydrogen reacted, \(\mathrm{H_2 + \tfrac12 O_2 \rightarrow H_2O}\):

    Quantity Expression λ = 2
    oxygen consumed \(0.5\) mol 0.5 mol
    air supplied (21 % O₂ by mole) \(n_{air} = 0.5\,\lambda / 0.21\) 4.76 mol
    dry gas leaving the cathode \(n_{dry} = n_{air} - 0.5\) 4.26 mol
    product water 1 mol 1 mol
    vapour the exhaust can carry \(n_{v,max} = \dfrac{y_{sat}}{1 - y_{sat}}\, n_{dry}\) 1.96 mol

    The exhaust can carry about twice the product water. If all of it evaporates, the exhaust leaves with a vapour mole fraction of 1/(1 + 4.26) = 0.19, a partial pressure of 28.5 kPa, i.e. 60 % relative humidity at 80 °C. That is below saturation, so nothing forces the water to condense. The energy to evaporate it, the latent heat \(\Delta H_{HHV} - \Delta H_{LHV}\) = 44.2 kJ/mol, is taken from the stack. It leaves with the exhaust instead of reaching the coolant.

    If the inlet air is humidified to a relative humidity \(\phi_{in}\) (at \(T_{op}\)), it already brings \(n_{w,in} = \dfrac{\phi_{in}\,p_{sat}}{P - \phi_{in}\,p_{sat}}\,n_{air}\) mol of vapour, and the capacity left for the product water shrinks. The vapour fraction of the product water is

    \[ x_v = \min\!\Big(1,\; \max\big(0,\; n_{v,max} - n_{w,in}\big)\Big) , \]

    computed by FuelCell.product_water_vapour_fraction at the cathode inlet humidity \(\phi_{in}\) of each operating point (FuelCell.cathode_inlet_rh). \(\phi_{in}\) is the larger of two sources:

    • a humidifier, EnergyConfig.fc_cathode_inlet_rh (relative humidity at the stack temperature; default 0, no humidifier);
    • the weather, EnergyConfig.fc_ambient_relative_humidity (relative humidity of the ambient air; default 0, dry ISA air). The compressor keeps the water mole fraction of the ambient air, so its partial pressure at the cathode is \(y_{w,amb}\,p_{cathode}\).

    The ambient state follows the mission ISA deviation, the input MissionConfig.disa (MissionInput['DISA'], default 0 K, stored as mission.DISA). The constraint diagram has its own deviation, ConstraintsConfig.disa. The weather has a small effect: saturated ambient air reaches the cathode at only 5 % relative humidity at 80 °C on an ISA day and 17 % at ISA+20 K, so all product water still leaves as vapour. On the hydrogen reference (examples/common.py) a saturated ISA+20 day changes MTOW by +0.02 % against a dry one, while the hot day itself adds 0.4 %. A humidifier is a different matter: with fc_cathode_inlet_rh = 1 the product water stays liquid (\(x_v\) = 0), and the take-off heat grows by 40 %, the TMS mass by 37 % and MTOW by 4.7 %. For the database stacks \(x_v\) = 1 up to \(\phi_{in}\) ≈ 0.5. It then falls quickly: 0.61 at 0.7, 0.36 at 0.8 and 0.08 at 0.9. A higher cathode pressure lowers \(y_{sat}\) and a lower \(\lambda\) lowers \(n_{dry}\); both reduce \(x_v\) (at λ = 1.5 the capacity is 1.41 mol). Above the boiling point at the cathode pressure (≈ 112 °C at 1.5 bar, e.g. a high-temperature PEM at 170 °C) the exhaust cannot saturate, and all product water is vapour.

    Two caveats. First, this is an equilibrium capacity at the stack exit. Locally, liquid water can still form (electrode flooding, cold spots), and the exhaust may leave sub-saturated, so \(x_v\) is an upper bound on the vaporised fraction. Second, what matters for the thermal-management system is the water state at the system exit, after any humidifier (which recovers water and heat from the exhaust) and the expander. That system-level water balance is not modelled yet. Set EnergyConfig.fc_product_water_vapour_fraction to a number (0 = all liquid, the HHV basis) to override the computed value. The hydrogen consumption does not depend on \(x_v\); only the heat sent to the TMS does.

    Left: the vapour fraction at the stack exit against the cathode inlet humidity. Right: the coolant heat per unit of gross electric power against the cell voltage, for liquid, half-vaporised and vapour product water.

    Because \(V_{\text{cell}}\) falls with load and altitude, the heat load peaks where the system works hardest. The weight loop sizes the thermal-management mass (WHeat_Exchanger) with one of two models, selected by TMSConfig(tms_class=...):

    • Class I (default): the peak heat over the mission and the take-off/OEI point, divided by a specific power (heat per kg of HEX), \(W_{\text{cool}} = Q_{\text{peak}} / \text{HEX specific power}\), with EnergyConfig.hex_specific_power_h2 defaulting to 5000 W/kg (vs ~1500 W/kg for a battery HEX rejecting to ambient — see Battery).
    • Class II: geometric coolant → LH2 and coolant → ram-air heat exchangers, a coolant pump (an electrical load on the fuel cell) and the ram-air duct drag (added to the polar), sized over the mission. See Thermal Management.

    A multi-MW fuel cell rejects a large heat load, so the TMS is a significant mass.

    5. Mission and weight closure¶

    Mission.HydrogenConfiguration integrates the hydrogen chemical energy over the mission, and the weight loop Weight.Hydrogen closes the take-off weight over

    \[ W_{TO} = W_{\text{struct}} + W_{\text{FC system}} + W_{\text{H}_2} + W_{\text{tank}} + W_{\text{cooling}} + W_{\text{payload}} + W_{\text{crew}} + W_{\text{reserve}} . \]

    The hydrogen load is the hydrogen energy of the flown profile and of the ground segments (taxi, take-off; see Mission) divided by Ef, increased by EnergyConfig.h2_fuel_margin (default 0.05: unusable/residual hydrogen and feed system). The final reserve (contingency_fuel, else 5 % of the load) is carried in the tank too: the tank is sized for load + reserve.

    Fuel-cell sizing is self-consistent and constraint-aware. Inside each weight iteration the stack is not sized to the mission peak alone: it is sized to the worst case of the mission peak, the take-off (field length) and the OEI climb — the latter two evaluated at the constraint conditions, since they are not flown by the mission profile but the fuel cell must still supply their power. To keep the flown fuel cell and the weighed fuel cell the same, the loop is SizeFromConstraint (seed) → fly → SizeForPropulsivePower(max(mission, take-off, OEI)) → re-fly until the required power converges. A single, explicit margin (FuelCell.SizingMargin, default 1.0) is applied — there are no hidden oversizing factors.

    See example 20_hydrogen_fuel_cell.py.

    6. Cryogenic LH2 tank¶

    Hydrogen is stored as a cryogenic liquid (~20 K). Systems/Tank/ provides LH2_Tank: structural + multi-layer-insulation (MLI) sizing (after Svensson et al.) and a transient time_step model of the tank thermodynamic state:

    • heat ingress through the insulation slowly boils the liquid and self-pressurizes the tank;
    • a vent valve opens at the maximum pressure \(P_{\max}\) (venting gas, a fuel loss);
    • a heater adds power at the minimum pressure \(P_{\min}\) to keep feed pressure up.

    It requires CoolProp (para-hydrogen properties) and is therefore optional: with a TankConfig and CoolProp installed it sizes the hydrogen storage of the 'Hydrogen' and 'FuelCellBattery' configurations (reporting the gravimetric index H₂/(H₂+tank), geometry and pressure limits; results().tank_mass is the empty tank-system mass). Without a TankConfig a simple gravimetric-index mass model (EnergyConfig.h2_gravimetric_index) is used. A TankConfig given without CoolProp raises a RuntimeWarning and falls back to the same gravimetric index (aircraft.tank is then None). A CoolProp failure inside the tank model (liquid density at sizing, properties in a time step) also warns and uses fixed fallback values.

    from PhlyGreen.config import TankConfig
config.tank = TankConfig(max_diameter=2.4, number_of_tanks=1, tank_model='Svensson_Default')

    TankConfig fields:

    field meaning
    max_diameter maximum tank outer diameter [m]. The inner radius is capped at max_diameter/2 − 0.15 m; the sized outer diameter is checked against max_diameter (ValueError if larger).
    number_of_tanks starting number of tanks; the automatic split adds tanks when that improves the gravimetric index, or when a cylinder would be longer than 20 inner radii.
    max_number_of_tanks cap on the split (default 4). At the cap the split stops; a cylinder longer than the aspect limit is then kept, with a warning.
    tank_model key into TANK_Database.
    fuselage_diameter not used by the model (kept for API compatibility): there is no fuselage-integration rule, so set max_diameter to the space available.

    The minimum pressure is floored at 1.2 bar: a database entry with a lower min_p_bar is raised to it with a warning. The ambient temperature of the heat leak is the package ISA (Utilities/Atmosphere.py) plus the mission DISA.

    To watch the tank state evolve over the mission, switch on tracking and re-fly (works for both 'Hydrogen' and 'FuelCellBattery'):

    aircraft.configure(config)                       # sizes the tank
aircraft.mission.track_tank = True
aircraft.mission.EvaluateMission(aircraft.weight.WTO)
from PhlyGreen import postprocess as pp
pp.plot_tank_state(aircraft)                      # pressure / mass / vent vs time

    The history (Mission.compute_tank_history) starts with the full tank (load + reserve) at the start of taxi-out and draws the same hydrogen as the sizing: taxi-out and the take-off run, the flown mission and taxi-in, at the flows of the ground segments. Time follows the profile clock (0 = start of the climb), so taxi-out and the take-off run have negative times. The end mass is the fuel margin + the reserve − the vented mass; the vented mass is not part of the sizing.

    Limitations. The following constants of Tank.py are unsourced engineering assumptions (marked as such in the code): the 0.15 m radial clearance under max_diameter, the 4 % ullage volume, the cylinder aspect limit L_cyl ≤ 20 r_inner, the 0.8 mm minimum gauge, the inner-wall design pressure 2.2 P_max, the 2.5× cylindrical jacket thickness, the cylinder accessory penalty 0.15 + 0.20 L_cyl/r_inner, the factors 1.3 (heat leak) and 2 in the pressure rate of time_step, the 1.2 bar floor on P_min and the default cap of 4 tanks. The tank is not integrated with the fuselage (no shielding, no fuselage resizing), and the thin-wall and buckling formulas have no fatigue, thermal-stress or support loads.

    See example 22_hydrogen_tank.py and notebook 03_hydrogen_fuel_cell.ipynb.

    7. Fuel cell + battery hybrid¶

    'FuelCellBattery' splits the propulsive power between the fuel cell and a (Class-I) battery via the profile's supplied-power ratio φ, which here is the battery share of the propulsive power, not of the supplied power as in Hybrid (reasons and conversion: Powertrain, Fuel cell + battery). The fuel cell uses its physics efficiency above; the battery is sized from its energy and power needs. The fuel cell is sized (by the same self-consistent loop as the pure-hydrogen path) to the worst of its own share (1−φ) of the mission peak, take-off and OEI — so a larger φ shrinks the stack, but never below the (1−φ) share of the take-off/OEI floor. Because the battery stores little energy per kg, hybridization helps short power-limited segments more than sustained cruise. A φ = 0 FuelCellBattery design reproduces a pure Hydrogen design exactly.

    When does a battery pay? A battery makes the aircraft lighter only if it lowers the power that sizes the fuel cell by more than its own mass (plus the snowball) costs. Example 23_fuelcell_battery_hybrid.py shows the two conditions on the ATR 42-600 reference, with the battery on the take-off run only (taxi_phi = 0):

    • The take-off must size the fuel cell. On the baseline 1,107 m field the climb needs more power than the take-off (3.0 vs 2.8 MW), so a take-off battery removes no stack mass and every battery share makes the aircraft heavier, whatever the battery. On an 800 m field (the ATR 42-600S short-field version) the take-off needs 3.9 MW against 3.4 MW for the climb.
    • The battery must be lighter per kW than what it removes. The battery shrinks the stack, its balance of plant and the TMS, but not the motor and the PMAD: the battery power flows through the same drive, which still carries the full take-off power. A battery on the take-off only (under a minute) is sized by its specific power. A battery that also covers the ~27-minute climb is sized by energy and never pays.

    On the 800 m field the power balance gives the take-off share at which the battery brings the fuel cell's take-off load down to its climb load, \(\varphi^* = 1 - P_{\text{climb}}/P_{\text{take-off}} = 0.145\), and the sweep finds the lightest design at φ = 0.15. Whether that design is lighter than pure hydrogen depends on the battery specific power (table: changes against the pure-hydrogen design, 24,024 kg, at φ = 0.15):

    Mass change [kg] 1.5 kW/kg pack (default) 3 kW/kg power pack (assumed)
    stack + BoP + PMAD −283 −382
    motor +6 −15
    TMS −145 −190
    battery +513 +246
    hydrogen +18 −4
    structure and the rest (snowball) +188 −380
    take-off weight +297 (+1.2 %) −726 (−3.0 %)

    A scan of the specific power at φ = 0.15 puts the break-even at about 1.75 kW/kg (−4 kg); at 5 kW/kg the aircraft is 1,112 kg (4.6 %) lighter. The 3 kW/kg and higher values are assumed technology levels for the study, not sourced pack data. Beyond φ* the climb sizes the fuel cell again and the battery only adds mass.

    8. Thermal management¶

    The fuel-cell heat is rejected by the thermal-management system, aircraft.tms: Class I (specific power) or Class II (geometric coolant → LH₂ and coolant → ram-air heat exchangers, pump and ram-air drag, sized over the mission). See Thermal Management and tutorial 08. Systems/Thermal/network.py also keeps a generic heat-source / heat-sink balance scaffold (HeatSource, HeatSink, HeatExchangerNetwork) for studies.

    9. Degradation (state of health)¶

    The stack carries a state of health relative to beginning of life (BOL). The defaults are BOL. Two descriptions of PEM degradation are supported, and they can be combined:

    Input (EnergyConfig) Legacy key Effect
    fc_voltage_loss [V] FC Voltage Loss uniform cell-voltage loss \(\Delta V\) at every current density
    fc_ohmic_resistance_factor FC Ohmic Resistance Factor \(R_{ohm} = k_R\,R_{ohm,BOL}\)
    fc_exchange_current_factor FC Exchange Current Factor \(i_* = k_i\,i_{*,BOL}\) (catalyst-area loss: \(k_i<1\))

    The uniform loss is the output of the empirical "voltage-decay rate × time at load" models (Pessot et al. 2020). The parameter drift follows the degradation models reviewed by Vichard et al. (2021). The degraded cell voltage is

    \[ V_{cell}(j) = V_{oc} - k_R R_{ohm,BOL}\,j - \eta_{act}(j;\,k_i\,i_{*,BOL}) - V_{conc}(j) - \Delta V . \]

    The state is used in two ways:

    • End-of-life sizing. Set the fields in the EnergyConfig of the design. The stack reaches the design voltage on the degraded curve, so it gets a lower design current density and a larger active area. The mass per area stays referenced to the BOL curve (PolarizationCurve(..., bol=True)), because the stack specific power is a hardware figure: the end-of-life stack is heavier, not lighter. The heat at a given power is larger, and so is the TMS.
    • Degraded flight of a sized aircraft. FuelCell.set_state_of_health(voltage_loss, ohmic_resistance_factor, exchange_current_factor) changes the state of a sized copy. Example 30 does this with fly_off_design(sized, range_nm, state_of_health={...}), and reports a violation when the stack is power-limited or the TMS cannot reject its heat (a Class-I TMS: the peak heat exceeds the heat it was sized for; a Class-II TMS: the frozen hardware falls short somewhere along the mission).

    Power-limited stack. When the demand exceeds the most the stack can deliver at a point, ComputePRatio runs the stack at its maximum-net-power point. The shortfall is supplied at that point's efficiency: the hydrogen and the heat are those of a proportionally larger stack running there. They stay physical and grow with the demand and with the degradation, and fuelcell._power_limited (see report_sizing()) marks the point as infeasible.

    Order of magnitude. The take-off-sized fuel-cell + battery design of example 30, sized at BOL, flies its nominal mission as follows with a degraded stack:

    Stack state Trip H₂ Peak heat Constraints violated
    \(\Delta V\) = 25 mV +5.2 % +17 % TMS heat
    \(\Delta V\) = 50 mV +11.7 % +62 % TMS heat; tank; MTOW
    \(k_R\) = 1.5 +4.4 % +37 % TMS heat

    The heat grows much faster than the hydrogen. At a given electrical power the hydrogen is proportional to \(1/V\), while the heat is proportional to \((V_{tn}-V)/V\). The TMS, not the tank, is therefore the first constraint to bind. A loss of 50 mV is about 10 % of a 0.5 V design voltage, a common end-of-life criterion. At that loss the stack is at the edge of its power capability: the beginning-of-life stack has an 11 % power margin (load ratio 0.889), which shrinks to nothing (0.9999).

    Degradation depends on the duty. Degradation rates depend on how the stack is loaded: time at each current level, load changes, start-stop cycles, idle and high-power operation. On the design above (whole profile, diversion included), the stack spends 78 % of the flight between 5 and 30 % of its design current density (0.72–0.82 V per cell), about 50 minutes above 0.80 V, and the 26-minute taxi at 4 % of the design current. The duty is therefore set by the sizing: a take-off-sized stack runs at low load for most of its life. Ageing models that turn a mission duty into a state of health are not part of the package. They can be built on the operating point that ComputePRatio exposes (i_op_last, v_cell_last) along mission.integral_solution and the ground segments. The battery state of health is described in Battery → State of health.
